Section 01

The Operations Dashboard Specification

A logistics dashboard earns its place by answering four questions at a glance: is anything late right now, is it getting more or less expensive to ship, is any single corridor underperforming, and is the trend improving. Everything below is scoped to answer exactly those four questions — nothing more, since a dashboard that tries to show everything ends up showing nothing clearly.

Core Panels
PanelShowsRefresh
Route MapActive shipment routes between origin and destination hubs, color-coded by on-time statusReal-time
On-Time RateRolling 7-day and 30-day on-time delivery percentage, by corridorHourly
Cost per ShipmentAverage fully-loaded cost per shipment, trended against the prior periodDaily
Delay AlertsAny shipment now projected to miss its delivery window, ranked by how far behindReal-time
Volume TrendShipment volume over time, by corridor and by carrierDaily
Build Notes for a Developer
  • Build the route map and delay alerts first — these are the panels people actually check daily; volume trend and cost reporting can follow in a second pass
  • Source on-time status directly from carrier tracking data or GPS feeds where available; don't rely on manual status updates, which decay in accuracy within weeks
  • Define "late" precisely before writing any code — see the KPI Definitions Reference in Section 05 — so the dashboard and the team are never arguing about what a red alert actually means
  • Keep the dashboard read-only for most users; route changes and exception handling should go through the workflow defined in the Governance Charter, not be edited directly on the dashboard
Scope discipline matters more than feature count. A five-panel dashboard everyone actually checks every morning is worth more than a twenty-panel dashboard nobody opens after the first week.
Section 02

The Business Plan Outline

This is the internal case for building an in-house logistics function — sized to be presented in a single meeting, not a fifty-page deck nobody reads before deciding.

Structure
  • Problem statement: name the specific, current pain — missed delivery windows, unpredictable freight costs, no visibility until a customer complains — in one paragraph, with real numbers from the last quarter
  • Cost baseline: what shipping costs today, per shipment and in total, including the hidden cost of manual tracking, expedited fixes, and customer service time spent on delay complaints
  • Expected impact: a conservative, sourced estimate — even a 10-15% improvement in on-time rate and a modest reduction in expedited-shipping costs pays for a first-phase build within a year for most mid-size operations
  • Phased investment: what phase one actually costs — developer time or a contractor's rate, plus any data or tracking integration fees — kept separate from the cost of scaling it company-wide later
  • Risk if nothing changes: a plain statement of what continues to happen if the status quo holds, since this is often the most persuasive paragraph in the whole plan
Keep the first version of this plan to two pages. A business case that requires fifty pages to justify a five-panel dashboard has already lost the argument.
Section 03

The Operations Governance Charter

A dashboard shows problems. It doesn't solve them. This charter defines who actually has authority to act — before the first real disagreement happens live, in front of a customer waiting on a shipment.

Decision Rights

Routing Decisions

  • Standard route changes: operations lead, same-day authority
  • Carrier changes affecting cost by more than 15%: requires manager sign-off
  • Emergency rerouting during an active delay: any trained dispatcher, logged after the fact

Exception Handling

  • Late shipment under 24 hours: dispatcher resolves directly with the carrier
  • Late shipment over 24 hours or a customer escalation: operations lead notified immediately
  • Repeated carrier failures on the same corridor: triggers an automatic quarterly carrier review

Dashboard & Data

  • Metric definitions: owned by operations lead, changed only with written notice to the full team
  • Dashboard access: read access for all operations staff, edit access limited to the system administrator
  • New panels or metrics: proposed by anyone, approved by operations lead

Vendor & Carrier Relationships

  • New carrier onboarding: requires operations lead and finance sign-off
  • Rate renegotiation: operations lead leads, informed by the cost-per-shipment trend data
  • Carrier termination: requires documented performance history from the dashboard
The point of writing this down is speed, not bureaucracy. A dispatcher who already knows they can reroute a delayed shipment without waiting for approval resolves the problem in minutes instead of hours.
Section 04

The Implementation Roadmap

A four-phase rollout, sized for a small operations team building this alongside their regular work — not a dedicated project team with unlimited time.

PHASE 1Weeks 1–3

Data Audit

Identify what tracking data already exists — carrier APIs, GPS feeds, manual logs — and what gaps need to be filled before any dashboard can show accurate information. Most of this phase is discovering that the data is messier than assumed.

PHASE 2Weeks 4–8

Pilot Corridor

Build the dashboard for a single route or region first — not the whole operation. Pick a corridor with decent existing data and enough volume to be meaningful, but small enough that mistakes are cheap to fix.

PHASE 3Weeks 9–12

Dashboard Deployment & Governance Adoption

Roll the dashboard out to the full pilot team, adopt the governance charter's decision rights for that corridor, and run it for a full month before expanding — this is where most of the real, unglamorous debugging happens.

PHASE 4Weeks 13+

Company-Wide Rollout

Expand corridor by corridor, using the pilot's actual on-time and cost improvements as the internal case for each new rollout — not projections, but real numbers from the pilot itself.

Section 05

The KPI Definitions Reference

Every team measuring "on time" or "delayed" slightly differently is one of the most common reasons a logistics dashboard loses trust within its first month. These definitions exist to be copied exactly, not adapted per team.

MetricDefinition
On-Time Delivery RatePercentage of shipments delivered within the promised delivery window, measured from the original commitment given at booking — not a window revised after a known delay.
Delay VarianceAverage difference, in hours, between promised and actual delivery time across all shipments in a period, including on-time shipments counted as zero.
Cost per ShipmentTotal fully-loaded shipping cost — freight, fuel surcharge, and handling — divided by total shipments in the period. Excludes one-time setup or integration costs.
Fuel Efficiency ImprovementPercentage change in fuel cost per mile shipped, comparing the current period to a fixed baseline period, adjusted for fuel price changes where data allows.
Margin ImpactEstimated effect of logistics performance on overall margin — calculated as cost savings from efficiency gains, minus any new operating costs from the system itself.
When a number looks wrong, check the definition first. Most dashboard trust problems come from two teams using the same metric name for two different calculations, not from a data or engineering bug.
